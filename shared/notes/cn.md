## I. Exponential and Poisson

### 1. 學習建議與理解重點 (Learning Advice and Key Concepts)

In network analysis, we frequently encounter events that occur at random intervals, such as connection requests arriving at a server or packets being processed by a router. Probabilistic models are essential tools that allow us to understand, quantify, and predict the behavior of these random network events. Mastering the Exponential distribution and the Poisson process is fundamental to analyzing network performance, as they provide the mathematical foundation for modeling key metrics like delay and packet loss, which are directly influenced by the stochastic nature of network traffic.

### 2. Core Concepts of Random Processes

#### 2.1. Exponential Distribution

The Exponential distribution is a continuous probability distribution that models the **time between events** in a process where events occur continuously and independently at a constant average rate.

- **Primary Applications:** In networking, it is commonly used to model the inter-arrival times for connections (`Exponential arrivals`) or the service times for packets (`Exponential durations`), especially when events are generated by a large number of independent users.
- **Probability Density Function (PDF):** The Probability Density Function (PDF), $f(t)$, describes the relative likelihood that the time until the next event will be _near_ `t`. The probability of the event occurring within a specific interval can be found by integrating the PDF over that interval. $f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t \ge 0$ Here, $\lambda$ represents the rate parameter, which is the average number of events per unit of time.

#### 2.2. Memoryless Property (無記憶性)

The memoryless property is the defining characteristic of the Exponential distribution. It states that the probability of a future event occurring is completely independent of how much time has already passed.

- **Implication:** If you are waiting for an event (e.g., the next packet arrival) and it hasn't happened yet, the probability distribution of the _remaining_ waiting time is identical to the original distribution. The process "forgets" its history.
- **Formal Definition:** $P(X > s+t | X > s) = P(X > t)$ for $s, t \ge 0$

#### 2.3. Poisson Process （泊松過程）

A Poisson process is a model used for **counting the number of events** that occur within a fixed interval of time or space. It is characterized by the following key properties:

- Events occur independently of one another.
- The average rate of event occurrences is constant over time.

#### 2.4. 🔗 概念連結：Exponential 與 Poisson 的關係 (Concept Link: Relationship between Exponential and Poisson)

The relationship between these two models is fundamental and symbiotic:

- If the **number of arrivals** in a given time interval follows a **Poisson distribution**, then the **time interval between consecutive arrivals** (the inter-arrival time) necessarily follows an **Exponential distribution**.

This powerful connection is why both models are cornerstones of queuing theory and network traffic modeling. They provide two perspectives on the same underlying random process: one counting events (Poisson) and the other measuring the time between them (Exponential).

#### 2.5. Advanced Properties (Based on Exam Outline)

- **Minimum of Multiple Exponential Processes:** If you have several independent Poisson processes with rates $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, ..., \lambda_n$, the time until the _very next_ event occurs (regardless of which process it comes from) also follows an Exponential distribution. The rate of this new combined process is simply the sum of the individual rates: $\lambda = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \lambda_i$. Furthermore, the probability that this next event comes from a specific process `i` is given by the ratio of its rate to the total rate: $P(\text{next arrival is from process } i) = \frac{\lambda_i}{\sum_{j=1}^{n} \lambda_j}$.
- **Superposition and Thinning:**
    - **Superposition (疊加):** Combining (or merging) multiple independent Poisson processes results in a new, aggregate process that is also a Poisson process. Its rate is the sum of the rates of the original processes.
    - **Thinning (稀疏化):** If events from a Poisson process are independently selected or discarded with a fixed probability `p`, the resulting stream of selected events is also a Poisson process, but with a new, lower rate ($\lambda' = p \lambda$).

### 3. 💡 考試提示 (Exam Tips)

- **Memoryless Property:** Understand its formal definition and, more importantly, its practical implication that past waiting time does not influence future waiting time.
- **Exponential-Poisson Relationship:** Be able to clearly articulate the link between event counts over time (Poisson) and the time between those events (Exponential).
- **Application:** Recognize how these models are applied to characterize connection arrivals and service times, which are the building blocks for analyzing queuing systems and overall network performance.

## II. Architecture

### 1. 學習建議與理解重點 (Learning Advice and Key Concepts)

Network architecture is best understood by comparing the two landmark systems that have shaped modern communications: the traditional telephone network and the Internet. The telephone network was engineered for a single application (voice) with guaranteed quality of service, leading to the development of circuit-switching. In contrast, the Internet was designed from the ground up to support a diverse and unknown range of applications by sharing resources efficiently, which gave rise to packet-switching. This comparison reveals the fundamental design philosophies and trade-offs that govern all communication networks.

### 2. The Telephone Network: Circuit-Switching

#### 2.1. Topology and Structure

To connect a large number of users efficiently, the telephone network evolved from impractical simple topologies (like a fully-connected mesh) to a scalable, **multi-level hierarchical topology**. This structure uses many short local connections and fewer high-capacity long-distance connections. The US telephone network structure is defined by key components:

- **LATA (Local Access and Transport Area):** A geographic area within which a local telephone company can provide service.
- **LEC (Local Exchange Carrier):** The company that handles calls within a LATA (local calls).
- **IXC (Interexchange Carrier):** A long-distance carrier that handles calls _between_ different LATAs.

#### 2.2. Multiplexing on Trunks

**Trunks** are high-capacity communication lines that connect switching offices and carry thousands of calls simultaneously. This is achieved through **multiplexing （多工）**, the technique of combining multiple signals for transmission over a shared medium. The two primary methods are:

- **FDM (Frequency Division Multiplexing):** Each call is allocated a unique, dedicated frequency band for the duration of the connection.
- **TDM (Time Division Multiplexing):** Each call is assigned a repeating time slot in a high-speed digital stream. For example, a stream might be divided into frames, with each call getting to use the first slot of every frame, another call getting the second, and so on.

#### 2.3. Circuit Switching (電路交換)

Circuit switching is a networking method where a dedicated, end-to-end communication path, or **circuit**, is established between two endpoints before the data transfer begins. All resources along this path (e.g., time slots in TDM or frequency bands in FDM) are reserved for the exclusive use of that connection for its entire duration, whether data is being sent or not.

### 3. The Internet: Packet-Switching

#### 3.1. Packet Switching (封包交換) vs. Circuit Switching

Packet switching is the fundamental communication method of the Internet. Messages are broken down into smaller, manageable blocks called **packets**. Each packet includes a header with addressing information and is sent independently through the network. Packets are reassembled at the destination to reconstruct the original message.

The table below critically evaluates the core differences between these two switching paradigms.

|   |   |   |
|---|---|---|
|Feature|Circuit Switching|Packet Switching (Datagram)|
|**Resource Allocation**|Dedicated path; resources reserved|Shared resources; statistical multiplexing|
|**Performance**|Synchronous, guaranteed QoS|Asynchronous, "best-effort"|
|**Connection Model**|Connection-oriented|Connectionless|
|**Reliability**|Inherently reliable connection|Unreliable (packets can be dropped)|
|**Suitability**|Ideal for constant-rate traffic|Efficient for bursty traffic|

#### 3.2. Internet Structure: A Network of Networks

The Internet is not a single, monolithic network but a decentralized "network of networks." Its hierarchical structure consists of:

- **Access ISPs (Internet Service Providers):** Provide connectivity to end-users (homes, businesses).
- **Regional ISPs:** Larger networks that connect multiple Access ISPs.
- **Tier-1 ISPs:** The "backbone" of the Internet, these are massive, global networks that peer with each other to provide worldwide connectivity.
- **IXPs (Internet Exchange Points):** Physical locations where different ISPs connect their networks to exchange traffic directly, rather than routing it through a third-party backbone.

#### 3.3. Performance Metrics

In packet-switched networks, performance is not guaranteed and is measured using metrics that are often **random variables** due to the effects of resource sharing and congestion.

- **Total Nodal Delay:** The total delay a packet experiences at a single router (node) is the sum of four components: $d_{nodal} = d_{proc} + d_{queue} + d_{trans} + d_{prop}$
    - `\(d_{proc}\)` (Processing Delay): Time to check the packet's header and determine its destination.
    - `\(d_{queue}\)` (Queuing Delay): Time spent waiting in the buffer (queue) before being transmitted. This is the only component that is highly variable and depends on congestion.
    - `\(d_{trans}\)` (Transmission Delay): Time required to push all of the packet's bits onto the link. It is `Packet Size / Link Bandwidth`.
    - `\(d_{prop}\)` (Propagation Delay): Time for a bit to travel from the start to the end of the link. It is `Link Length / Propagation Speed`.
- **Analogy:** Think of a convoy of cars (the packet) traveling down a highway (the link). **Transmission delay** is the time it takes for the entire convoy to get onto the highway past the entrance ramp. It depends on the number of cars (packet size) and the speed of the ramp (link bandwidth). **Propagation delay** is the time it takes for the _first car_ to travel from the entrance ramp to the exit ramp. It depends only on the length of the highway and the speed limit.
- **Professor's Note:** Understanding this decomposition is crucial. While propagation and transmission delays are often fixed by physics and hardware, **queuing delay is the primary battleground for performance management.** It is the direct symptom of congestion, and nearly all advanced networking protocols, which we will study later, are designed to control or mitigate it.
- **Packet Loss (封包遺失):** This occurs when a packet arrives at a router and finds the input buffer (queue) is completely full. The router has no choice but to discard ("drop") the packet.
- **Throughput (吞吐量):** The actual rate at which bits are successfully transferred between a sender and receiver. The end-to-end throughput is limited by the **bottleneck link （瓶頸鏈路）**—the slowest link along the path. For a server connected at rate `\(R_s\)` and a client at rate `\(R_c\)`, the throughput is $\min(R_s, R_c)$.

#### 3.4. Layering and Encapsulation (分層與封裝)

- **Layering （分層）:** The complex tasks of networking are broken down into a series of distinct layers, each providing a specific service to the layer above it. This **layered architecture** helps manage complexity and promotes modularity, allowing individual layers to be updated without affecting shared/references/others. The Internet uses a 5-layer stack:
    1. **Application:** Supports network applications (HTTP, SMTP).
    2. **Transport:** Provides end-to-end data transfer (TCP, UDP).
    3. **Network:** Handles routing of datagrams from source to destination (IP).
    4. **Link:** Data transfer between neighboring network elements.
    5. **Physical:** Transmission of raw bits over the medium.
- _Note: While we use a 5-layer model for simplicity, some models further distinguish between the Application protocols themselves (e.g., HTTP) and the Content they carry (e.g., web pages), which corresponds to OSI layer 7._
- **Encapsulation （封裝）:** As data moves down the protocol stack at the source, each layer "wraps" the data received from the layer above it by adding its own header information. For example, the Transport layer takes an application message and adds a TCP header to create a _segment_. The Network layer then takes this segment and adds an IP header to create a _datagram_. This process is reversed at the destination.

#### 3.5. Congestion (壅塞)

Network congestion occurs when the demand for a network resource, such as link bandwidth or router buffer space, exceeds its available capacity. This directly impacts the performance metrics discussed earlier. Specifically, as demand approaches capacity, the **queuing delay (**`**\(d_{queue}\)**`**) grows non-linearly, and router buffers begin to fill, leading to packet loss.**

### 4. 💡 考試提示 (Exam Tips)

- **Circuit vs. Packet Switching:** This is a fundamental concept. You must know the core trade-offs regarding resource allocation, efficiency for different traffic types, and performance guarantees.
- **Components of Nodal Delay:** Be able to identify, define, and differentiate between the four types of delay a packet can experience at a router.
- **Layering and Encapsulation:** Understand _why_ networks use a layered architecture and be able to describe the process of encapsulation.
- **Internet Hierarchy:** Know the roles of different ISP tiers (Access, Regional, Tier-1) and Internet Exchange Points (IXPs) in forming the global Internet.

## III. Application Layer

### 1. 學習建議與理解重點 (Learning Advice and Key Concepts)

The Application Layer is the top layer of the protocol stack and serves as the direct interface between network services and user-facing applications. This layer's protocols define the rules and message formats for specific network tasks, such as web browsing (HTTP), email (SMTP), and file sharing. Crucially, these protocols do not manage the end-to-end data delivery themselves; instead, they rely on the underlying services provided by the Transport Layer—primarily the choice between the reliable TCP and the fast, best-effort UDP.

### 2. Application Architectures and Services

#### 2.1. Architectural Models

- **Client-Server Model （主從式架構）:** This is a centralized model where an "always-on" host, the **server**, provides services and waits for requests. **Clients** are end-user devices that initiate communication by sending requests to the server and waiting for a response.
- **Peer-to-Peer (P2P) Model （點對點模型）:** This is a decentralized model with minimal or no reliance on a dedicated server. Each participant, or **peer**, can act as both a client and a server, communicating directly with other peers to request and provide content or services. This architecture is highly scalable.

#### 2.2. Underlying Transport Services

Applications choose a transport protocol based on their specific requirements for reliability, timing, and throughput.

- **TCP (Transmission Control Protocol):** Provides a **reliable, connection-oriented** service. It guarantees that all data arrives in order and without errors, and it includes mechanisms for flow control to avoid overwhelming the receiver.
- **UDP (User Datagram Protocol):** Provides an **unreliable, connectionless** "best-effort" service. It does not guarantee delivery, order, or error-free transmission. Its advantage is its low overhead and speed.

Note the direct mapping between an application's needs and its choice of transport. HTTP and Email cannot tolerate loss, making TCP's reliable service mandatory. In contrast, VoIP, which can tolerate a 'small amount' of loss but is highly sensitive to delay, forgoes TCP's retransmission overhead in favor of UDP's speed.

### 3. Key Application Layer Protocols

#### 3.1. HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol)

HTTP is the foundational protocol of the World Wide Web, used by web browsers to request and transfer files (like HTML pages, images, etc.) from web servers.

- **Connection Types:**
    - **Non-Persistent HTTP:** Establishes a new TCP connection for _every single object_ (the main HTML file, each image, etc.) on a web page. This involves significant overhead due to repeated TCP connection setup (multiple Round-Trip Times).
    - **Persistent HTTP:** After the initial TCP connection is established, multiple objects can be requested and sent over that single, persistent connection. This significantly improves performance by reducing the connection setup overhead.
- **Statefulness and Cookies:**
    - HTTP is a **stateless protocol （無狀態協定）**. This means the server does not retain any information about past requests from a client. Each request is treated as an independent transaction.
    - To create a sense of state for applications like e-commerce (e.g., remembering a user's shopping cart), **cookies** are used. The server sends a small piece of data (the cookie) to the client's browser. The browser stores it and sends it back with every subsequent request to that server, allowing the server to identify the user and recall their past activity from a database.

#### 3.2. Email Protocols (SMTP, POP3, IMAP)

The Internet email system consists of **User Agents** (e.g., Outlook, Gmail web interface) and **Mail Servers**. Three key protocols manage the flow of email:

- **SMTP (Simple Mail Transfer Protocol):** SMTP is a **"push" protocol**. It governs two main phases of email delivery: first, from the sender's user agent to their mail server (**a client-server interaction**), and second, from the sender's mail server to the recipient's mail server (**a server-to-server interaction**). It is used exclusively for sending email.
- **Mail Access Protocols (POP3 and IMAP):** These are **"pull" protocols** used by a recipient's user agent to retrieve messages from their mail server.
    - **POP3 (Post Office Protocol):** A very simple protocol designed to download emails from the server to a single local client. The typical workflow is to connect, retrieve all mail, store it on the local device, and then delete it from the server.
    - **IMAP (Internet Mail Access Protocol):** A more powerful and complex protocol that allows users to access and manipulate messages directly on the mail server. It maintains state, allowing users to create folders, and view the same mailbox consistently across multiple clients (e.g., a phone and a laptop).
- **Comparison of SMTP and HTTP:** While both use TCP, their nature is different. HTTP is primarily a "pull" protocol where the client requests files from a server. In contrast, SMTP is a "push" protocol where the client (or sending server) pushes files to a destination server.

### 4. 💡 考試提示 (Exam Tips)

- **Architectures:** Be able to clearly distinguish between the centralized Client-Server model and the decentralized Peer-to-Peer (P2P) model.
- **HTTP State and Connections:** Understand why HTTP is fundamentally stateless, how cookies are used as a mechanism to create state, and the performance differences between persistent and non-persistent HTTP connections.
- **Email Protocol Roles:** Know the specific and distinct function of each protocol: **SMTP** for pushing/sending, **POP3** for simple downloading/retrieving, and **IMAP** for accessing/managing mail on the server.
- **Transport Choices:** Understand the trade-offs that lead an application to choose TCP (when reliability is essential) versus UDP (when low latency and speed are more important than guaranteed delivery).
